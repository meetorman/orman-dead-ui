Sunday, May 8, 1977 — Mother’s Day. The Grateful Dead played Barton Hall, a fieldstone drill hall on the Cornell University campus rated for 5,000 people, on the night of a hard freeze: the campus weather station logged a high of 54°F, a low of 27°F, and about two inches of snow before its next morning reading.c18 c17 s62

Student tickets were $6.50 in advance; the door was $7.50. Betty Cantor-Jackson recorded the night on her own two-track mix. In 2011 the Library of Congress placed the recording in the National Recording Registry — the only Grateful Dead concert held there — and in 2017 Cornell University Press published a book about the show.c19 c20 c11 c6 c34

II.

The Night

01The booking

Barton Hall went up during the First World War as a military drill hall — fieldstone walls, crenellations, an interior long enough for artillery practice — and it served the campus ROTC before it served anything else. Cornell’s administration had been wary of rock concerts since a near-riot at a Deep Purple show in 1973. The student Concert Commission booked the Dead anyway, working with the New Jersey promoter John Scher, whose standard contract put the loss of a failed show on Scher rather than on the students.c18 c27 c21 b·25-48

Advance tickets for Cornell students were $6.50, limited to two per ID; everyone else paid $7.50. The poster and handbill were drawn by Jay Mabrey, a graduating senior. The show sold out.c19 c20 c31 b·47-48

02The day

The band came in from Boston Garden — the middle date of three, between Kreutzmann’s birthday show on the 7th and Buffalo on the 9th. Jon Reis, on freelance assignment for the Ithaca Journal, photographed the band’s Cadillac limousines drawing up to the hall’s arched door. Inside, Larry Reichman’s camera caught the stage going up in the empty hall. At the afternoon sound check a student called for “Funiculì, Funiculà,” the 1880 song written for the Vesuvius funicular; the band played it, and within two weeks it had entered their repertoire.c22 b·49-65

The hall was rated for 5,000. Estimates of the crowd that night run from 6,000 to 8,500. When the doors opened, the crush at the front frightened people.c18 c17 b·49-65

At the turnstiles, ticket-taker Stephen Burke admitted roughly 250 people who had no tickets — not for money, which was forbidden, but for a song, a joke, or an object he judged sufficient. One man offered a peanut butter sandwich wrapped in tin foil.b·52

Backstage, Brad Krakow remembers Keith Godchaux asleep before the show, and Bill Kreutzmann asking after a painkiller for an injured wrist.c45 c5 b·90-91

03The show

Twelve songs in the first set, a few minutes under ninety. The opener was “New Minglewood Blues,” Garcia on the aluminum-necked Travis Bean TB500. For Mother’s Day, Weir sang Merle Haggard’s “Mama Tried” — a convict’s song: the mother tried, the sentence is life. When it ended, Lesh leaned into his microphone: “Thanks, Mom.”c23 c24 b·61-82

Between sets the hall turned tropical, and Garcia — who almost never spoke from the stage — asked the floor to give way.c25 b·78

The second set ran six songs and roughly eighty minutes, with no drum solo — one of the few nights of 1977 without one. “St. Stephen” returned after nearly five years, run into “Not Fade Away” and back again — about twenty-three minutes without a break. The set closed with “Morning Dew,” written by Bonnie Dobson in 1961 as a dialogue between the last man and the last woman after a nuclear war. The Dead had carried it since 1967.c5 c3 b·78-95

The encore was “One More Saturday Night.” Weir thanked the room and said good night.b·95

04The exit

The campus station read 27 degrees that night, with 0.0 inches of snow recorded for May 8. Its gauge is read once each morning, so anything that fell overnight was booked to May 9 — about two inches by that reading. What the instrument cannot resolve is the hour the snow began.s62 c100 c101

The half-foot is not in the record. The two inches are.c7842

05The tape

Betty Cantor-Jackson mixed the night to her own two-track, from her own console, split from the stage feed and independent of the house sound. Her husband, crew chief Rex Jackson, had died the previous September; she worked the 1977 tour alone.c11 b·31 b·94-106

By 1986 she could not pay storage on her belongings, among them roughly a thousand reels of the band’s live tape. The band did not bid. In 1987 the lots went to public auction, and a high-school chemistry teacher paid $100 for three weathered road cases because he wanted the cases. The reels sat in a barn in northern California until December 1995, when the engineer Rob Eaton began restoring them — more than seven hundred hours, reel by reel. Copies spread; within a year the Cornell tape was among the most copied in circulation.c14 c13 c12 b·100-106 c2

The Library of Congress added the show to the National Recording Registry in 2011, crediting in part the audience reels of the taper Jerry Moore. The band recovered the master tapes only at the end of 2016. Rhino released the show on May 5, 2017 — three days short of forty years.c6 c7 c10 c8

The Tape’s Passage

  1. Spring 1977 — Betty Cantor-Jackson runs an independent two-track mix of each show from her own console: New Haven 5/5, Boston 5/7, Cornell 5/8, Buffalo 5/9.c11 b·94-106
  2. July 1977 — taper Jerry Moore’s audience recording reaches vault archivist Dick Latvala; it becomes the most-traded version of the show.c15 c16
  3. 1986–87 — storage default; the reels are seized and auctioned. A high-school chemistry teacher pays $100 for three weathered road cases.c14 b·100-106
  4. December 1995 — Rob Eaton begins a 700-hour restoration of the flood-damaged reels; the “Betty Boards” re-enter circulation.c12 b·100-106
  5. 2011 — the Library of Congress selects the recording for the National Recording Registry, citing in part Moore’s audience reels.c6 c7
  6. End of 2016 — the band secures the master tapes. May 5, 2017 — official release (3-CD / 5-LP) and the four-show box “May 1977: Get Shown the Light.”c10 c8 c9
